## CASE FILE 001 · POINT PLEASANT, WEST VIRGINIA · EST. POPULATION 4,500

## THE MOTHMAN

For thirteen months a seven-foot figure with glowing red eyes stalked a small river town — and everyone who chased the truth found the truth chasing them back.

## ARTIST'S RECONSTRUCTION · WITNESS ACCOUNTS

## POINT PLEASANT · 1966

## A MONSTER… OR A WARNING?

Late on November 15, 1966, two young couples — Roger and Linda Scarberry and Steve and Mary Mallette — were driving through the abandoned TNT area north of Point Pleasant when their headlights caught something standing by the road: a gray figure the size of a man, near seven feet tall, with folded wings and two red eyes that threw the light straight back like an animal's. It spread its wings and rose without a running start. As they raced for town it flew after the car, shrieking, matching them mile for mile at speeds they later swore touched a hundred — until it broke off at the city limits. At the courthouse, deputies found four grown adults too rattled to finish a sentence. Within days a dozen more came forward with the same impossible account, and then dozens after that. The papers had a name for it by the weekend, and a small-town scare became an American legend.

## THE VANISHING DOG

Two nights before that famous drive, contractor Newell Partridge was watching television near Salem when the picture dissolved into a herringbone of static and, outside, his generator began to whine. His German shepherd, Bandit, locked onto something in the hay field and started to howl — a sound Partridge said he'd never heard the dog make. He swung a flashlight across the field and caught two red circles glaring back, like "bicycle reflectors." Bandit bolted straight for them into the dark. He never came home. When Partridge read, days later, about a creature "with red eyes" out at the TNT area, he understood exactly where his dog had run — and what he'd run toward.

## WHERE IT WATCHED

The "TNT area" earned its name honestly. During the Second World War the tract was the West Virginia Ordnance Works, a plant that manufactured and packed TNT, then stashed it inside dozens of domed concrete "igloos" hidden through the woods so no single blast could take the rest. By 1966 the plant sat abandoned: a honeycomb of bunkers, tunnels, and man-made ponds, swallowed by second-growth forest and posted against trespass. It was, in other words, the single best place in the county for something large to live unseen. The land is now the McClintic Wildlife Management Area — and the igloos are still out there, doors rusted open.

## HOW IT GOT ITS NAME

No one in Point Pleasant ever called it "the Mothman." That name was minted in a newsroom. In the winter of 1966 the campy Batman television series was a coast-to-coast craze, and a copy editor working the wire desk — hunting for something with more bite than "the Bird" — reached for the villain flavor of the moment and filed it as Mothman. It ran on front pages from Ohio to New York within days. The creature, whatever it was, never announced a name of its own. We handed it one, and the brand outlived every witness. "It was shaped like a man, but bigger. And those eyes — they were like automobile reflectors, and they were red. I've never seen anything like it."

## ROGER SCARBERRY · TO THE POINT PLEASANT POLICE · NOVEMBER 1966

## THE SIGHTINGS GROW

The story ran, and the reports came with it. Over thirteen months more than a hundred people — farmers, volunteer firemen, a National Guardsman, whole families driving home from church — described the same thing in the same words: a broad- winged shape that lifted straight up with no running start, moved in complete silence, and left a witness sick with dread that seemed to arrive before the creature did. Skeptics note the descriptions grew more uniform as the coverage spread. Believers note they were uniform from the very first night, before there was anything to copy.

## THE REPORTER

Across the river, Mary Hyre of the Athens Messenger chased every sighting when the bigger papers wouldn't — and, she said, it began chasing her. Strange men in dark suits turned up at her office asking flat, rehearsed questions and warning her, plainly, to stop writing. Her own niece, Connie Carpenter, had seen the thing up close and came away with eyes that burned and watered for days. Hyre kept filing anyway. Her columns are the closest thing this case has to a contemporaneous record — and she paid for every inch of them.

## REPORTED BY MARY HYRE · ATHENS MESSENGER

## LOCAL LEGEND · THE CORNSTALK CURSE

Old-timers had a different explanation, older than any monster. In 1777 the Shawnee chief Cornstalk was murdered while held under a flag of truce at Fort Randolph — the ground Point Pleasant now stands on. As he died, the story goes, he laid a curse on the land for two hundred years. Two centuries later, almost to the season, the bridge came down. To some in town the Mothman never caused a thing. It was the curse, arriving on schedule — and the creature was only there to watch.

## THE NIGHT IT TOUCHED SOMEONE

The next evening — November 16, 1966 — twenty-one- year-old Marcella Bennett drove out to the TNT area to visit the Thomas family, her infant daughter Tina in her arms. As the group crossed back to their car, something that had been crouched low beside it rose to its full height right in front of them. Bennett later described it in the words that would come to define the case: "a big gray thing, bigger than a man, with terrible glowing red eyes." She froze, then collapsed — and as she fell she dropped the baby. A cousin snatched the child off the ground and shoved everyone back inside while the creature crossed onto the porch and pressed its face to the window. Bennett said the fear didn't leave for months. She spoke of that night until her death in 2015.

## A TOWN ALREADY MARKED

Point Pleasant was uneasy ground long before 1966. On October 10, 1774, Virginia militia and the Shawnee under Chief Cornstalk fought a daylong battle here at the meeting of the Kanawha and the Ohio — a fight local tradition still bills as "the first battle of the American Revolution." Three years later Cornstalk returned under a flag of truce and was murdered by soldiers at Fort Randolph. The town, in other words, was built on a battlefield and a broken promise. Whatever you believe stalked the TNT area, it arrived in a place that had been keeping score for two hundred years.

## ON THE RECORD · THE WORD "PROPHECY"

Point Pleasant's own paper first ran the story straight: four terrified adults, one impossible bird. But readers noticed the reports didn't stay simple. Witnesses spoke of dread that landed before the creature appeared, of dreams of "water and Christmas lights," of a feeling that something was coming. By the time author John Keel arrived, he had stopped calling them sightings. He called them warnings.

## THE STRANGER THINGS

The winged figure was only the surface. In the same thirteen months Point Pleasant filled with the inexplicable, and the deeper investigators dug, the stranger it got. Telephones rang at all hours with garbled voices, metallic tones and stuttering clicks no operator could trace. Lights the size of dinner plates drifted low across the fields and hung, motionless, over the TNT igloos. And again and again came the Men in Black — pale, oddly-dressed strangers in spotless dark cars who asked residents unnerving questions about conversations they could not possibly have overheard, then drove off and were never identified. On November 2, 1966, a sewing-machine salesman named Woodrow Derenberger said a craft shaped like "a kerosene lamp chimney" settled onto Route 2 in front of his truck. A grinning man stepped out, introduced himself — without moving his lips — as Indrid Cold, and claimed to come from a place he called Lanulos. "We mean you no harm," Cold told him. Author John Keel, who moved to Point Pleasant to run the investigation himself, gathered every thread of it — the bird, the calls, the lights, the strangers, the prophecies of disaster whispered down phone lines — and reached a conclusion that still unsettles: for thirteen months, this ordinary river town had become a doorway, and the Mothman was standing in the frame.

## THE BRIDGE COLLAPSE

At 4:58 p.m. on December 15, 1967 — thirteen months to the day after the first sighting — the Silver Bridge sat bumper-to-bumper with Christmas rush hour when it simply came apart. In under a minute the entire span dropped into the freezing Ohio River. Forty-six people died; two were never recovered. Thirty-one vehicles went down with it. Investigators traced the whole catastrophe to a single point: eyebar 330, one steel link near the top of the Ohio tower. A crack just one-tenth of an inch deep — invisible from a passing car — had spread by stress-corrosion for decades until the link fractured clean. In an eyebar-chain design, one broken link is all it takes; the rest unzips. The failure was so total it rewrote federal law, forcing the 1968 Federal-Aid Highway Act and the first national bridge-inspection standards in American history.

## THE PROPHECIES ON THE LINE

What pulls this case out of the ordinary monster file is what happened to the people investigating it. As John Keel dug in through 1967, he said the phenomenon began speaking back. Phones rang with warnings. Contacts relayed premonitions of a great catastrophe — a disaster tied to the holidays, "water," and a power failure that would black out much of the country around Christmas. Keel braced for a blackout that never came. Instead, on December 15, the bridge fell. He spent the rest of his life convinced he had been handed the date and simply misread the shape of the warning. That single conviction — that the sightings were a countdown, not a haunting — is why the world remembers this as a book of prophecies and not just of a bird.

## THE EYE THAT BURNS

Several witnesses reported more than fright. After close encounters, some — Connie Carpenter among them — suffered days of red, swollen, streaming eyes, a reaction doctors compared to the burn welders get from staring at an arc. Keel logged the same complaint again and again and could never explain it: the creature's eyes, people insisted, did something to their own. Whatever the red glow was, more than one person in Point Pleasant carried it home in their face. "Point Pleasant is not unique. There are 'window' areas all over the world where these things concentrate — and the events always seem to build toward something."

## JOHN A. KEEL · THE MOTHMAN PROPHECIES · 1975

## THE THEORIES

## UNKNOWN ANIMAL

WVU wildlife biologist Robert L. Smith argued it was a sandhill crane — a bird nearly five feet tall with a seven-foot wingspan and red skin around the eyes, blown far off its normal range. A snowy owl shot nearby in 1966 is still mounted in the museum.

## MASS HYSTERIA

## A WARNING

## SOMETHING ELSE

## THE MOTHMAN SINCE

The creature never fully left the culture. Believers point to reports of a huge black-winged shape seen by workers at Chernobyl in the days before the 1986 disaster — the so-called "Black Bird" — and to sightings claimed before other tragedies since. Whether that's pattern or wishful thinking, the Mothman had by then escaped Point Pleasant entirely: Keel's 1975 book made it a national myth, and the 2002 film starring Richard Gere fixed it in pop culture for good.

## THE TOWN TODAY

Point Pleasant chose to embrace its monster. A 12-foot polished-steel statue by sculptor Bob Roach went up downtown in 2003. The Mothman Museum opened in 2006. And every September the Mothman Festival pulls 10–12,000 visitors into a town of 4,500 — proof that a good mystery outlasts everyone who lived it.
